Squaring the Circle, Kepler’s Triangle & the Pyramids¶

Circle–Square Harmonics and the \((1, \sqrt{\phi}, \phi)\) Right Triangle

Since Egyptian rope-stretchers and Greek geometers first sought a square equal in perimeter or area to a given circle, squaring the circle has stood at the threshold between algebraic constructibility and transcendental measure.

Exact Impossibility vs. Remarkable Harmonic Resonance¶

By Lindemann’s theorem (1882), \(\pi\) is transcendental over \(\mathbb{Q}\): no finite sequence of unmarked straightedge and compass intersections (geometor.model) can construct a segment of exact length \(\sqrt{\pi}\) or \(\frac{\pi}{4}\).

Yet when we examine the Kepler Right Triangle—the unique right triangle whose side lengths are in geometric progression—an extraordinary near-resonance between \(\phi\) and \(\pi\) appears:

The Kepler Golden Right Triangle \((1, \sqrt{\phi}, \phi)\)¶

Suppose a right triangle has legs \(1\) and \(x\) and hypotenuse \(x^2\) in geometric progression \(1 : x : x^2\). By the Pythagorean theorem (or rational quadrance law \(Q_1 + Q_2 = Q_3\)):

\[1 + x^2 = x^4 \implies (x^2)^2 - x^2 - 1 = 0 \implies x^2 = \phi, \quad x = \sqrt{\phi}\]

Thus the quadrances of the Kepler triangle are \((1,\, \phi,\, \phi^2 = \phi + 1)\)—the Golden Ratio itself embodied as a right triangle!

  • In geometor/etc, the Kepler Golden Right Triangle (1, √φ, φ) serves as one of our four canonical reference triangles alongside Acute (5,6,7), Egyptian Right (3,4,5), and Kimberling (6,9,13).

  • Rotating two Kepler triangles back-to-back along their \(\sqrt{\phi}\) altitude forms the meridian cross-section traditionally associated with the Great Pyramid of Giza: base \(2\), apothem (slant height) \(\phi\), and altitude \(\sqrt{\phi}\) (slope angle \(\arctan(\sqrt{\phi}) \approx 51^\circ 49' 38''\)).

Perimeter and Area Circle–Square Proportions¶

Consider a circle of radius \(r = \sqrt{\phi}\) (altitude of the pyramid) and a square of side \(s = 2\) (base of the pyramid):

  • Square perimeter: \(P_{\square} = 4 \times 2 = 8\).

  • Circle circumference with radius \(\sqrt{\phi}\): \(C_{\bigcirc} = 2\pi\sqrt{\phi} \approx 7.9923\dots\) — agreeing with \(P_{\square}\) to within \(0.096\%\) (reflecting the classic algebraic approximation \(\pi \approx \frac{4}{\sqrt{\phi}} \approx 3.1446055\dots\))!
