Scholastic Noetics, Renaissance Proportion & Celestial Harmony¶

Thomas Aquinas · Fra Luca Pacioli & Leonardo da Vinci · Johannes Kepler · Blaise Pascal

St. Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274)¶

  • GEOMETOR Edition: aquinas — Opera de Scientia Mathematica et Materia Intelligibili (Repo)

In Super Boetium De Trinitate (Questions 5 and 6) and his commentaries on Aristotle’s Posterior Analytics, Physics, De Anima, and Metaphysics, Aquinas articulated how mathematics stands uniquely poised between natural physics and pure metaphysics: mathematical judgment abstracts from sensible qualities while terminating in the imagination’s intelligible matter (materia intelligibilis)—pure continuous extension in which geometric necessity is verified without empirical uncertainty.

Fra Luca Pacioli (c. 1447–1517) & Leonardo da Vinci (1452–1519)¶

  • GEOMETOR Edition: pacioli — De Divina Proportione (1509) (Repo)

Franciscan mathematician Luca Pacioli—steward of Piero della Francesca’s polyhedral geometry and mathematical collaborator with Leonardo da Vinci at the court of Milan—composed De Divina Proportione (completed 1498, printed in Venice in 1509). Celebrating Euclid’s Extreme and Mean Ratio through its thirteen admirable effects, architectural proportions, Roman majuscule letterforms, and Leonardo’s 60 unprecedented solid (*solido*) and skeletal (*vacuo*) perspective woodcuts of the Platonic, Archimedean, and stellated polyhedra, Pacioli gave the Golden Ratio its Renaissance name.

Johannes Kepler (1571–1630)¶

  • Featured in: etc (Kepler Reference Triangle) · Squaring the Circle, Kepler’s Triangle & the Pyramids

In Mysterium Cosmographicum (1596) and Harmonices Mundi (1619), Kepler famously declared:

“Geometry has two great treasures: one is the Theorem of Pythagoras; the other, the division of a line into extreme and mean ratio. The first we may compare to a measure of gold; the second we may name a precious jewel.”

Kepler proved that consecutive Fibonacci ratios converge to \(\phi\), classified the convex and star polyhedra, and identified the Kepler Right Triangle \((1, \sqrt{\phi}, \phi)\)—which unites both “great treasures” in a single triangle and serves as one of the four canonical reference triangles across geometor/etc.

Blaise Pascal (1623–1662)¶

  • Featured in: pappus (Hexagrammum Mysticum)

At age sixteen, Pascal generalized Pappus’s two-line hexagon theorem to any six points inscribed on a non-degenerate conic (Essai pour les coniques, 1640): opposite sides of an inscribed hexagon meet in three collinear points along a Pascal line. Permuting six vertices yields the 60 Pascal lines, 20 Steiner points, 60 Kirkman points, and 15 Plücker lines of the Hexagrammum Mysticum—verified symbolically in exact SymPy algebra inside geometor/pappus.
