Medieval Transmission, Renaissance Proportion & Classical Triangle Statics¶

Fibonacci · Aquinas · Nicholas of Cusa · Pacioli & Leonardo · Dürer · Kepler · Pascal · Ceva · Euler & Feuerbach

Leonardo Pisano — Fibonacci (c. 1170 – c. 1250)¶

  • GEOMETOR Edition: fibonacci — Liber Abaci (1202) & Practica Geometriae (1220) (Repo)

Pisan mathematician whose Liber Abaci introduced Hindu–Arabic positional arithmetic, unit-fraction decompositions, and the additive Fibonacci recurrence \(1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21, \dots\) to Latin Europe, while his Practica Geometriae transmitted exact Euclidean and Ptolemaic radical measurements of the regular pentagon, decagon, and icosahedron over \(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5})\).

St. Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274)¶

  • GEOMETOR Edition: aquinas — Opera de Scientia Mathematica et Materia Intelligibili (Repo)

In Super Boetium De Trinitate (Questions 5 and 6) and his commentaries on Aristotle’s Posterior Analytics, Physics, De Anima, and Metaphysics, Aquinas articulated how mathematics stands uniquely poised between natural physics and pure metaphysics: mathematical judgment abstracts from sensible qualities while terminating in the imagination’s intelligible matter (materia intelligibilis)—pure continuous extension in which geometric necessity is verified without empirical uncertainty.

Nicholas of Cusa (1401–1464)¶

  • GEOMETOR Editions: cusa — De Docta Ignorantia (1440) & De Mathematicis Complementis (1453) (Repo)

German cardinal, jurist, and philosopher whose De Docta Ignorantia (On Learned Ignorance, Book I) employed exact transsumptive mathematical symbolism to contemplate the Infinite: as radius grows without bound, the curved circumference approaches coincidence with its straight tangent (coincidentia oppositorum)—inspiring Kepler (“divinus mihi Cusanus”), Desargues’s points at infinity, and modern limiting geometry.

Piero della Francesca (c. 1415–1492)¶

  • GEOMETOR Edition: piero — Libellus de Quinque Corporibus Regularibus (Repo)

Master painter and mathematician of Borgo Sansepolcro whose Libellus de Quinque Corporibus Regularibus (Codex Vaticanus Urbinas latinus 632) computed exact quadratic and golden radical coordinates, reciprocal dual inscriptions, and Archimedean truncations of the five Platonic solids.

Fra Luca Pacioli (c. 1447–1517) & Leonardo da Vinci (1452–1519)¶

  • GEOMETOR Editions: pacioli — De Divina Proportione (1509) · Summa de Arithmetica (1494) (Repo)

Franciscan mathematician Luca Pacioli—steward of Piero della Francesca’s polyhedral geometry and mathematical collaborator with Leonardo da Vinci at the court of Milan—published both the encyclopedic Summa (1494) and De Divina Proportione (1509). Celebrating Euclid’s Extreme and Mean Ratio through its thirteen admirable effects, architectural proportions, Roman majuscule letterforms, and Leonardo’s 60 unprecedented solid (*solido*) and skeletal (*vacuo*) perspective woodcuts of the Platonic, Archimedean, and stellated polyhedra, Pacioli gave the Golden Ratio its Renaissance name.

Albrecht Dürer (1471–1528)¶

  • GEOMETOR Edition: durer — Underweysung der Messung (1525) (Repo)

Master printmaker and geometer of Nuremberg whose Underweysung der Messung mit dem Zirckel und Richtscheyt (1525) founded German constructive geometry: introducing parallel-projection conic sections (anticipating Monge’s descriptive geometry), unfolded polyhedral paper nets (Netze), the classic fixed-compass pentagon, modular Roman and Fraktur letterforms, and mechanical perspective frames.

Johannes Kepler (1571–1630)¶

  • GEOMETOR Edition: kepler — Mysterium (1596), Strena (1611) & Harmonices Mundi (1619) (Repo) · etc (Kepler Reference Triangle)

In Mysterium Cosmographicum (1596), Strena seu de Nive Sexangula (1611), and Harmonices Mundi (1619), Kepler famously declared:

“Geometry has two great treasures: one is the Theorem of Pythagoras; the other, the division of a line into extreme and mean ratio. The first we may compare to a measure of gold; the second we may name a precious jewel.”

Kepler nested the five Platonic solids among the planetary spheres, discovered the rhombic dodecahedron, formulated the sphere-packing conjecture \(\pi/(3\sqrt{2})\), connected Fibonacci ratios to botanical phyllotaxis, classified the 11 Archimedean tilings and star polyhedra, identified the Kepler Right Triangle \((1, \sqrt{\phi}, \phi)\), and discovered the Harmonic Third Law \(T^2 \propto a^3\).

Blaise Pascal (1623–1662)¶

  • GEOMETOR Edition: pascal — Essay pour les Coniques (1640) (Repo)

At age sixteen, Pascal generalized Pappus’s two-line hexagon theorem to any six points inscribed on a non-degenerate conic (Essay pour les Coniques, 1640): opposite sides of an inscribed hexagon meet in three collinear points along a Pascal line. Permuting six vertices yields the 60 Pascal lines, 20 Steiner points, 60 Kirkman points, and 15 Plücker lines of the Hexagrammum Mysticum.

Giovanni Ceva (1647–1734)¶

  • GEOMETOR Edition: ceva — De lineis rectis se invicem secantibus statica constructio (1678) (Repo)

Milanese and Mantuan geometer who elevated Archimedes’ Law of the Lever into pure incidence geometry: by placing virtual statical weights at the vertices of a triangle (and tetrahedron), Ceva proved Ceva’s Concurrency Theorem (\(\frac{AF}{FB}\cdot\frac{BD}{DC}\cdot\frac{CE}{EA} = 1\)) alongside Menelaus’s dual—planting the direct conceptual seed of Möbius’s homogeneous barycentric coordinates powering geometor/etc.

Leonhard Euler (1707–1783)¶

  • GEOMETOR Edition: euler — Solutio facilis (E325, 1765) (Repo)

In St. Petersburg memoir E325 (Solutio facilis, 1765), Euler computed the exact algebraic distances among the four classical centers of any scalene triangle—discovering \(OI^2 = R^2 - 2Rr\) and proving the \(2:1\) collinearity of the Euler Line (\(X_4, X_2, X_3\)).

Lorenzo Mascheroni (1750–1800)¶

  • GEOMETOR Edition: mascheroni — La Geometria del Compasso (1797) (Repo)

Pavian mathematician and poet whose La Geometria del Compasso (1797) proved that every Euclidean ruler-and-compass construction—including circle division and straight-line intersection—can be executed cleanly with the compass alone.

Karl Wilhelm Feuerbach (1800–1834)¶

  • GEOMETOR Edition: feuerbach — Eigenschaften (1822) (Repo)

In Nuremberg (Eigenschaften einiger merkwürdigen Punkte des geradlinigen Dreiecks, 1822), Feuerbach proved that the circle through the side-midpoints and altitude-feet (center \(X_5\), radius \(R/2\)) touches the incircle internally at the Feuerbach point \(X_{11}\) and all three excircles externally—inaugurating modern triangle geometry.
